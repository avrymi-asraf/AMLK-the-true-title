\begin{abstract}
HeSum is one of the few corpora large enough to train modern Hebrew news summarizers, but its targets
are extended subheadings scraped from news sites rather than summaries written for modeling. We audit
whether those targets are suitable references before treating them as ground truth. A reproducible
pipeline filters unusable rows and rewrites defective headlines; an independent rubric judge then
scores original and curated headlines directly against their articles for faithfulness, single-focus,
informativeness, and cleanliness. The audit removes $41.5\%$ of the source corpus and rewrites $52.4\%$
of the retained headlines. Flagged references show large, defect-specific quality losses, while curated
headlines are preferred in $73.6\%$ of blind comparisons. These results show that scraped reference
quality is a first-order experimental variable, not a
fixed property of a benchmark. We release the resulting audit and frame target validation as a
prerequisite for trustworthy Hebrew summarization training and evaluation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

HeSum \citep{paz-argaman2024hesum} is one of the few public resources large enough to fine-tune a
modern Hebrew news summarizer. Its ``summaries,'' however, are extended subheadings scraped from news
sites, not references written for the task. Such targets can bundle several stories, contain boilerplate,
or assert details absent from the paired article. Training and evaluating against them as if they were
gold therefore risks rewarding a model for reproducing dataset defects.

Current summarization practice usually fine-tunes on the available corpus and reports overlap metrics
such as ROUGE or BERTScore \citep{zhang2020bertscore}. Both assume the reference is trustworthy.
That assumption is especially fragile for scraped targets: lead-derived references can reward copying
the opening sentence rather than summarizing the article \citep{nallapati2016abstractive,see2017get}.

The problem became visible during qualitative inspection of model outputs. Fluent generated headlines
were sometimes more focused and better grounded than the HeSum headline used to score them. Returning
to the raw corpus revealed recurring, source-driven patterns rather than isolated annotation mistakes:
pipe-delimited digests joined unrelated headlines, roundup pages paired one article body with claims
about several stories, and extraction artifacts survived in both article text and target. These cases
made a model-only diagnosis inadequate. If the target itself is defective, improving architecture or
optimization cannot make reference-based evaluation trustworthy.

We reverse the usual order: audit the references first, then train. We contribute (i) a reproducible
pipeline that filters unusable HeSum rows and repairs defective headlines, (ii) an article-grounded,
four-dimension rubric that evaluates references without requiring a second reference string, and
(iii) independent graded and pairwise tests of whether the flagged rows are genuinely worse and the
rewrites genuinely better. The same evaluation stack also defines a controlled training comparison between models fine-tuned on
the uncleaned HeSum data versus the curated corpus. The audit is the completed claim of this paper;
the training comparison is retained as a modular final experiment while its two arms finish.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

HeSum \citep{paz-argaman2024hesum} contains $10{,}000$ real-world Hebrew news article--headline pairs.
Our pipeline first trims
repeated byline and related-link tails from $722$ articles. Two deterministic filters then run
independently: the token-budget filter flags $2{,}659$ rows whose article and headline exceed
$4{,}000$ DictaLM tokens, and the multi-pipe filter flags $2{,}412$ headlines containing at least two
pipe separators. Their intersection leaves $6{,}486$ rows before any model decision is introduced.

An LLM curator (\texttt{gpt-5.6-luna}) assigns each survivor one source-usability label with explicit
negative guards: usable, multiple independent items, insufficient substantive content, substantive
content absent from the text, damaged or fragmentary text, or other. It removes $632$ unusable rows,
then either keeps or rewrites each usable headline. This separation matters: deterministic exclusions
do not depend on the curator, and source usability is decided before headline wording is repaired. The
final corpus contains $5{,}854$ rows, of which $2{,}785$ retain their original headline and $3{,}069$
receive a rewrite. Figure~\ref{fig:funnel} gives the complete flow and Table~\ref{tab:strata} defines the
overlapping analysis groups used below.

The stratum numbering deliberately omits S1 (over-token-budget). Because the filter is a hard length
cut, S1 and clean rows have no common support in article length and cannot support a valid group
comparison. We instead analyze length continuously as a supplementary lead-bias check
(Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:leadbias}). For E4, the curated corpus splits $80/10/10$ by article into
train/validation/test ($4{,}683$/$585$/$586$ rows). One arm trains on that curated train split; the other
trains on a size-matched sample of uncleaned HeSum after excluding the curated validation and test
IDs. Both arms share that curated test split; generation and scoring use a fixed seeded subset of
$120$ test articles (not the full $586$).

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\paragraph{Article-grounded rubric.}
String-overlap metrics cannot establish whether a lone reference is correct. We therefore use Gemini
\texttt{gemini-2.5-flash-lite} to read the article and score a headline from 1--5 on
\emph{faithfulness} (every claim is supported), \emph{single-focus} (one dominant event rather than a
bundle), \emph{informativeness} (the reader can identify what happened), and \emph{cleanliness} (no
scraping or formatting artifacts). Keeping the dimensions separate prevents a structurally messy
headline from being treated as factually wrong, and lets each filter predict a specific failure
signature.

The judge is blind to stratum and provenance: it never sees whether a headline is original, curated, or
expected to fail. It also belongs to a different model family from the curator, reducing self-preference
risk \citep{panickssery2024llm}. Twelve Hebrew worked examples---scores 1, 3, and 5 for each
dimension---anchor the ordinal scale and are excluded from downstream analysis. On the pilot,
quadratically weighted test--retest agreement is $0.86$ for faithfulness, $0.90$ for single-focus,
$0.76$ for informativeness, and $0.65$ for cleanliness; a human spot-check is reported in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

Four pre-registered experiments use this instrument to answer four separate questions: are the flagged
strata actually worse (E1); does repairing a headline improve its own score (E2); do blind readers agree
(E3); and does training on the curated corpus rather than the uncleaned data change a model's output
(E4).

\paragraph{E1: Are the flagged strata worse than clean headlines?}
E1 scores $9{,}988$ original headlines and compares each flagged stratum with clean S0 using
Mann--Whitney tests with Holm correction and Cliff's $\delta$ \citep{cliff1993dominance}. Directional
predictions are fixed before scoring: for example, multi-pipe headlines should primarily damage
single-focus and cleanliness, not faithfulness. Because the strata overlap, we avoid an omnibus test
whose independence assumption would be violated. Effect size and bootstrap confidence intervals carry
the interpretation; with thousands of rows, statistical significance alone is uninformative.

\paragraph{E2: Does the paired repair raise a headline's own score?}
E2 compares original and curated scores on $3{,}068$ rewritten pairs with Wilcoxon signed-rank tests and
reports the proportions that improve, tie, or regress.

\paragraph{E3: Do blind readers prefer the repaired headline?}
Because a five-point scale compresses changes near its ceiling, E3 presents both headlines in randomized
order to a blind pairwise judge and checks position bias. It evaluates a rewritten cohort and an
unchanged placebo cohort: because original and curated strings are identical in the placebo, any
systematic preference there would expose judge bias rather than a curation effect.

\paragraph{E4: Does training on the curated corpus change model quality?}
Two arms fine-tune the Hebrew-native Mistral-7B model \dictalmtwo{} with LoRA
\citep{hu2022lora} ($r=32$, $\alpha=64$, dropout $0.05$; attention and MLP projections) under matched
optimization: completion-only cross-entropy loss, one epoch, effective batch size $16$, cosine learning
rate peaking at $2\times10^{-4}$, bf16 precision, and greedy decoding. The arms use different training
datasets of equal size ($4{,}683$ rows): Arm~A on uncleaned HeSum and Arm~B on the curated corpus.
Held-out curated validation and test IDs are excluded from the uncleaned sample so evaluation articles
are not seen in training. Both arms share the curated test split ($586$ rows), the same chat-formatted
instruction, a $4{,}096$-token sequence budget, a $128$-token decode budget, and a Hebrew-script decode
constraint. Generation and scoring use a fixed seeded subset of $120$ articles from that test split,
matched by construction across arms. Outputs are scored on the same rubric dimensions and compared
blindly head to head. Final E4 results will occupy the reserved paragraph in
Section~\ref{sec:e4-results}.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{Flagged references are genuinely worse (E1).}
Every pre-registered directional prediction is confirmed (Figure~\ref{fig:e1delta}). The largest
effect is S2 single-focus ($\delta=-0.95$, median 1 vs.\ 5): multi-pipe headlines collapse on exactly
the dimension the filter targets. S2 and S3 also lose more faithfulness than predicted
($\delta=-0.85$ and $-0.43$), showing that bundled headlines often assert content absent from the paired
article. S2 informativeness also falls sharply ($\delta=-0.81$), so the defect is not merely unusual
punctuation: a reader cannot reliably recover one central event. By contrast, S4 originals remain close
to clean S0 (largest $|\delta|=0.27$), exposing a meaningful disagreement between the curator and
independent judge rather than hiding it. The curator appears more willing to rewrite stylistically weak
headlines than the rubric is to assign them low ordinal scores. These are shape shifts, not just lower
averages: S0 sits at ceiling throughout, while S2's single-focus mass spreads across the full range
(Figure~\ref{fig:rubricdist}).

\paragraph{The rewrites improve the target (E2/E3).}
On $3{,}068$ paired rewrites, faithfulness increases for $42.0\%$ of rows and decreases for $3.6\%$;
the transition matrix shows that movement is overwhelmingly upward (Figure~\ref{fig:transition}).
Because medians remain compressed at the top of the five-point scale, the blind comparison provides
the clearer magnitude: curated headlines win $73.6\%$ of rewritten comparisons
($n=961$, 95\% CI $[70.8\%,76.4\%]$), while unchanged placebo pairs tie $99.5\%$ of the time
($n=198$; Figure~\ref{fig:pairwise}). The placebo result makes instrument noise an implausible
explanation for the preference. The benefit is concentrated in substantive edits: full rewrites win
$76.8\%$ of comparisons ($n=833$), whereas light edits are near a coin flip ($49.5\%$, $n=97$).
Thus curation is not uniformly beneficial merely because text changed; it helps most where the original
target required reconstruction.

E1, E2, and E3 answer different questions -- validating the labels, tracking which dimensions move after
repair, and measuring preference without ceiling compression -- and their agreement supports a narrow
claim: the pipeline identifies real reference defects and improves the headlines it rewrites, without yet
establishing downstream model gain, which is why E4 remains separate.

\paragraph{Training comparison (E4; reserved).}
\label{sec:e4-results}
The controlled Arm~A--B run (uncleaned versus curated training data) is still completing. This
paragraph is intentionally reserved for the final rubric and blind-preference comparison; no claim
about training on the curated corpus is made from a single arm. The completed audit and repair
findings above do not depend on E4.

\section{Limitations and Conclusion}
\label{sec:limitations}
This audit shows that HeSum's scraped headlines cannot be treated as fixed ground truth: reference
quality varies systematically with observable extraction patterns (E1) and repair measurably improves it
(E2/E3). The methodological implication is that this can reverse how a model score should be read --
matching a bundled or unsupported headline more closely is not necessarily better summarization, so
evaluation should ask whether the target itself is faithful before asking whether an output resembles it.
A single-annotator spot-check (Figure~\ref{fig:humancheck}) shows the judge tracks a human reader within
one point on the ordinal rubric, and this check is being extended across the full annotator team.

Two caveats bound this claim. Curator and judge come from different model families and neither is a human
gold standard, and the curator's editorial style leaks into what counts as a repair -- it rewrote many
headlines the judge already scored near clean (S4), so a preferred rewrite is not proof of the unique
correct target. The audit also only shows that repairing retained headlines improves them, not that every
filtered article should be discarded or that training on the curated corpus yields a better model;
E4 addresses the latter. Reference auditing should still precede fine-tuning and benchmarking alike
-- a scraped target is an observation to validate, not ground truth by default.
